 # Candidate Assessment Guidelines

## Scoring framework

Assign one score based on the candidate's demonstrated position on the proposition:

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `-2` | Strongly opposes the proposition |
| `-1` | Somewhat opposes the proposition |
| `0` | Explicitly neutral, mixed, or genuinely balanced |
| `+1` | Somewhat supports the proposition |
| `+2` | Strongly supports the proposition |
| `null` | Insufficient evidence to assess |

`null` must not be treated as `0`. An unknown position is not a neutral position.

## Scoring interpretation

Use the following behavioural distinctions when assigning directional scores:

| Score | Suggested criterion |
| --- | --- |
| `-2` | Explicit, repeated, or substantial opposition; clear commitment to prevent or reverse the proposition |
| `-1` | Partial opposition, qualified rejection, or opposition with significant exceptions |
| `0` | Explicit neutrality, genuinely balanced support and opposition, or an explicitly conditional position without a clear directional preference |
| `+1` | Partial support, qualified endorsement, or support with significant exceptions |
| `+2` | Explicit, repeated, or substantial support; clear commitment to implement the proposition |
| `null` | Evidence does not establish a sufficiently reliable position |

These are evidence-based guidelines, not mechanical rules. A single explicit commitment may justify `+2`; multiple weak statements do not automatically justify it.

## Candidate versus party positions

- Party policy may provide context, but cannot automatically establish an individual candidate's position.
- A candidate's explicit statement takes precedence over an inferred position from party membership.
- Where a candidate has no individual position, party policy may only be used if the assessment is explicitly labelled as party-derived rather than candidate-confirmed.

This distinction is particularly important for individual electorates.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer evidence in this order:

1. Official policy documents.
2. Candidate statements.
3. Parliamentary voting records.
4. Reliable secondary reporting.

Use the highest-ranking relevant sources available. Record the source and a direct link or other identifying information for every assessment.

## Evidence freshness

- Prefer current statements and policies.
- Record publication dates where available.
- Flag outdated evidence.
- Reassess an assessment when a candidate changes position or policies change materially.

## Interpreting parliamentary votes

Parliamentary votes are not always straightforward evidence of support for a proposition. Amendments, procedural votes, bundled legislation, and party discipline can complicate their interpretation.

A voting record should support a score only when the vote's substance is sufficiently clear and its relationship to the proposition can be reliably established. If the substance or relationship is unclear, disclose the uncertainty and use `null` where the position cannot be assessed reliably.

## Assessment requirements

- Every score must have traceable evidence supporting the assessment.
- Assess the candidate's position on the actual proposition, not assumptions based on their party affiliation, ideology, or general reputation.
- If evidence is conflicting, ambiguous, outdated, or insufficient, disclose that uncertainty. Do not silently resolve conflicts; use `null` when the position cannot be assessed reliably.
- Apply identical evidence standards, scoring criteria, and treatment of uncertainty across all candidates and parties.
- Distinguish an explicit neutral or balanced position (`0`) from a lack of evidence (`null`).
